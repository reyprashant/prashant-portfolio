Quantum computing represents a quantum leap in the art of computation. It promises to perform calculations that are extremely difficult, or practically impossible, for classical digital computers to complete efficiently.

This article looks at the history of computing, the fundamental principles of quantum computing, what has been achieved so far, and the challenges that lie ahead.

## The evolution of computers

Computing has gone through three fundamental stages.

### 1. Analog computers

Pioneered by Charles Babbage and other early innovators, mechanical computing machines laid the groundwork for every computational device that followed.

Analog computers use continuously varying physical quantities to represent and solve mathematical problems. Historically, such systems were used to simulate physical phenomena, including the motion of celestial bodies.

### 2. Digital computers

Alan Turing's conceptualisation of the Turing Machine gave modern computing its theoretical foundation.

Digital computers operate on binary bits, represented as 0s and 1s. They have transformed every industry through their speed, reliability and efficiency, carried by the rapid advance of transistor technology.

### 3. Quantum computers

Quantum computers draw on the principles of quantum mechanics, a field advanced significantly by physicists such as Richard Feynman.

Unlike classical bits, quantum bits, or _qubits_, can exist in superpositions of the states 0 and 1. This property lets quantum computers process information differently from classical machines, and potentially solve certain complex problems much more efficiently.

## What are quantum computers?

Quantum computers use quantum-mechanical systems, such as atoms, ions or superconducting circuits, to represent and manipulate information.

Where a classical computer processes information through binary states, a quantum computer uses qubits that can exist in a superposition of states. That opens up new ways of attacking problems that involve enormous amounts of data and complex mathematics.

### Understanding superposition

One of the fundamental principles of quantum computing is **superposition**.

In classical computing, a bit represents either 0 or 1 at any given moment. In quantum computing, a qubit can exist in a combination of both states until it is measured.

The best-known illustration is **Schrödinger's cat**, a thought experiment used to show how strange quantum superposition is. The experiment is theoretical, but it helps explain how quantum systems behave differently from the objects of everyday life.

## How much faster are quantum computers?

One of the most common questions about quantum technology is simple: how much faster is a quantum computer than a digital one?

The answer depends on the problem being solved.

Digital computers process information with bits, each either 0 or 1. Quantum computers use qubits that can exist in superpositions of those states.

Two classical bits represent one of four combinations at a time:

- 00
- 01
- 10
- 11

Two qubits can exist in a superposition involving all four of those basis states at once. As the number of qubits grows, the mathematical space that describes the system grows exponentially.

That does not mean a quantum computer automatically performs every calculation exponentially faster. Its advantage depends on specialised quantum algorithms that exploit superposition, interference and entanglement. For the right problems, though, a quantum computer may significantly outperform even the most powerful classical machines.

## What has been achieved so far?

### Quantum supremacy

Quantum supremacy, also called quantum computational advantage, is the demonstration of a quantum processor completing a computational task beyond the practical reach of classical computers.

In 2019, Google announced a milestone using its **Sycamore** processor, which ran 53 operational qubits during the experiment. Google reported that the processor finished a specialised task in about 200 seconds, and estimated that a leading classical supercomputer would need around 10,000 years for the same task.

That comparison was later challenged as researchers developed better classical simulation techniques. Even so, the experiment stands as an important milestone in quantum computing research.

IBM, Microsoft, Amazon and Google have continued to develop quantum processors, error-correction techniques and the infrastructure around them. Together, that work shows steady progress toward more powerful and more reliable quantum systems.

## Will quantum computers replace digital computers?

The short answer is **no, at least not in the foreseeable future**.

Quantum computers are complex, expensive and difficult to operate. They face serious engineering challenges, including sensitivity to their environment and the need for sophisticated error correction.

Classical computers remain extremely good at everyday tasks: browsing the web, running applications, managing databases and processing business information.

Quantum computers are more likely to complement classical systems by taking on specialised problems that conventional architectures find exceptionally hard. Rather than replacing digital computers, quantum computing is expected to extend what computation can do.

## What problems can quantum computers solve?

Quantum computing could contribute to several important scientific and technological fields.

### 1. Unstructured data and complex analysis

Quantum algorithms may offer new approaches to certain mathematical, optimisation and data-analysis problems. Whether they bring practical advantages for general unstructured data processing is still an active research question.

### 2. Supply chains and logistics

Quantum computing could improve certain optimisation problems in supply chain management, transport networks, scheduling and resource allocation, helping organisations explore complex combinations of decisions more efficiently.

### 3. Weather forecasting

Weather forecasting needs sophisticated mathematical models and enormous computing resources. Quantum computing may eventually contribute to scientific simulation and numerical modelling, although practical advantages for operational forecasting have not yet been shown.

### 4. Drug discovery and medical research

One of the most promising applications is the simulation of molecules and chemical interactions. Better molecular simulation could support drug discovery and the development of new treatments for diseases such as cancer.

Quantum computers are not a cure for anything by themselves, but they may become valuable tools for the researchers looking for one.

### 5. Encryption and cryptography

Quantum computing could reshape cybersecurity. A powerful, fault-tolerant quantum computer could break some of the public-key cryptographic systems in wide use today.

That prospect has driven the development of post-quantum cryptography, designed to protect digital information against future quantum attacks.

## What are the challenges?

### Decoherence

The biggest obstacle facing quantum computers is **quantum decoherence**.

Quantum systems are extremely sensitive to their surroundings. Heat, electromagnetic interference and other noise can disturb the delicate quantum states that computation depends on. When those interactions make quantum information lose its useful coherence, errors follow.

Keeping coherence demands carefully controlled environments and advanced engineering. Many quantum platforms run at temperatures close to absolute zero to reduce thermal noise, although not every quantum technology needs that.

Beyond decoherence, researchers have to solve qubit stability, scalability, error correction and the considerable cost of building and running these machines. Overcoming all of this is what stands between today's experiments and large-scale, fault-tolerant quantum computers.

## Conclusion

Quantum computing is a remarkable step forward in how we understand computation. By drawing on quantum mechanics, it offers new ways to solve problems that may sit beyond the practical reach of classical computers.

The field has reached significant research milestones, but it still faces major challenges, above all decoherence, error correction and scale.

Quantum computers are unlikely to replace traditional digital computers. They are more likely to become specialised tools for important scientific and computational problems.

As this quantum era unfolds, the meeting of quantum mechanics and computer science will keep opening new ground for research and discovery.

**The future of computing may not be classical or quantum, but both.**
